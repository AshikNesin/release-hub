# Owning Play Console doesn't switch off Play Protect

Owning a Play Console account gives a developer **no switch, manifest flag or API that exempts their own APKs from Play Protect's install-time scan**. In AOSP, verification is removed only for ADB test installs. For every other PackageInstaller session the framework clears the "disable verification" flag, and it verifies updates just as it verifies fresh installs ([AOSP PackageInstallerService](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/main/services/core/java/com/android/server/pm/PackageInstallerService.java), [AOSP VerifyingSession](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/main/services/core/java/com/android/server/pm/VerifyingSession.java)). Only four things genuinely make the prompt go away. The first is the device owner turning off "Scan apps with Play Protect". The second is installing over ADB with `verifier_verify_adb_installs=0`. The third is installing into a work profile or onto a fully managed device, where Google says the prompt "will not appear". The fourth is letting the Play Store be the installer, through internal app sharing or a testing track; this one is inferred, not documented. Everything else only helps the scan *pass* or avoids the harder blocks. That includes 2026 developer verification, package and key registration, Play Protect appeals, dropping sensitive permissions and targeting a current SDK. Google itself says appeals "won't remove" the unknown-app prompt ([Play Protect warning guidance](https://developers.google.com/android/play-protect/warning-dev-guidance)). For Release Hub this has a concrete consequence. Each new version is a new, unseen APK, so silent `USER_ACTION_NOT_REQUIRED` auto-updates can still stop at a Play Protect dialog. The installer app's own permission set, which pairs `REQUEST_INSTALL_PACKAGES` with `UPDATE_PACKAGES_WITHOUT_USER_ACTION`, matches a November 2026 report of a verified, registered app that was hard-blocked as harmful anyway. The practical setup for personal devices is to keep Play-tracked apps on Play and confine Release Hub's installs to a work profile, or else turn scanning off on dedicated devices. Registering the direct-channel signing key now prepares for global verification in 2027.

## Three separate gates produce the dialogs you are seeing

The dialogs the question lists come from three mechanisms, and each one needs a different remedy. The first is a **"familiarity" gate**. "App scan recommended" ("Play Protect hasn't seen this app before", with Scan app / Don't install) and "Send app for security check" ("This app is unknown to Play Protect") are documented with a single trigger: Play Protect does not know the APK ([Play Protect warning guidance](https://developers.google.com/android/play-protect/warning-dev-guidance)). If the user agrees, Play Protect uploads signals for a "real-time code-level scan" and returns "appears safe" or "potentially harmful" ([Play Protect client protections](https://developers.google.com/android/play-protect/client-protections)). Google announced this behaviour in October 2023 and rolled it out country by country ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2023/10/18/google-play-protect-scan/)). Google publishes no install-count threshold. The help page says developers "may be asked to send each new version" ([Google Play Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/answer/2812853)), which implies the unit of "seen" is the specific build, not the package or the key. That inference is supported by an October–November 2026 report from Harbor, a self-updater using `UPDATE_PACKAGES_WITHOUT_USER_ACTION` with a stable key: "App scan recommended" appeared on *every* update, even with "Improve harmful app detection" turned on ([harbor #88](https://github.com/RDevNeo/harbor/issues/88), anecdotal).

The second is a **policy gate**, and a scan does not lift it. "Harmful app blocked" follows a PHA or Mobile Unwanted Software classification. "App blocked to protect your device", the enhanced fraud protection block, stops apps installed from "Internet-sideloading sources (web browsers, messaging apps or file managers)" that declare `RECEIVE_SMS`, `READ_SMS`, notification-listener or accessibility permissions ([CSA Singapore](https://www.csa.gov.sg/news-events/press-releases/google-partners-with-the-cyber-security-agency-of-singapore-to-pilot-a-new-enhanced-security-feature-to-better-protect-android-mobile-users-from-scams/), [Play Protect warning guidance](https://developers.google.com/android/play-protect/warning-dev-guidance)). That block began in Singapore in 2024 and had reached about ten countries, including India and Brazil, by early 2025 ([Business Standard](https://business-standard.com/technology/tech-news/google-play-protect-s-new-tools-enhances-app-security-on-android-details-125013000970_1.html)). No official current country list exists. Google also does not say whether a dedicated installer app like Release Hub counts as an "internet-sideloading source".

The third is the **identity gate**, Android developer verification. It asks *who* published the app, not whether the app is safe, and Google says it collects nothing about app content ([verification FAQ](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification/guides/faq?hl=en)). One anecdotal October 2026 report shows a hybrid message, "Play Protect hasn't seen an app from this developer before", on an app declaring only `INTERNET` ([Castlegame #296](https://github.com/abolfazl2600/Castlegame/issues/296)). The reporter attributes it to verification, though Google says sideloads are not yet in scope (see below). A fourth system, live threat detection, runs on Pixel 6+ *after* install and has no install-time prompt to skip ([Google Security Blog](https://security.googleblog.com/2024/11/new-real-time-protections-on-Android.html)).

## Only four routes actually remove the prompt, and none of them is a developer opt-out

The table separates options that truly skip the prompt from those that only change the outcome. The confidence column says whether the claim rests on Google documentation or AOSP source (Official), on reasoning from how the mechanism works (Inferred), or on developer reports (Anecdotal).

| Option | Effect on the scan prompt | Scope and cost | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turn off **Play Store → Play Protect → Settings → "Scan apps with Play Protect"** | **Skips**: the install-time verifier stops | Per device and user-controlled. Also turns off Play Protect's malware scanning for all apps. Unknown whether the fraud-permission block also turns off | Official ([Google Play Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/answer/2812853)) |
| `adb shell settings put global verifier_verify_adb_installs 0` (Developer options "Verify apps over USB") | **Skips**, but **only for `adb install`** | Developer machines only. Overridden if a device policy controller sets `ENSURE_VERIFY_APPS`. `pm install --skip-verification` works only for updates of *debuggable* apps | Official ([AOSP Settings.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/main/core/java/android/provider/Settings.java), [AOSP VerifyingSession](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/main/services/core/java/com/android/server/pm/VerifyingSession.java)) |
| Install into a **work profile** or onto a **fully managed** device | **Skips the prompt**. On-device scanning continues | Needs an EMM or an Android Management API enterprise. Personal-profile installs still prompt | Official ([Android Enterprise Help](https://support.google.com/work/android/answer/15162069)); a third-party expert dates the change to Sept 2024 ([bayton.org](https://bayton.org/android/android-enterprise-faq/play-protect-sideload-scanning-change/)) |
| Play as installer: **internal testing track**, **internal app sharing**, **managed Google Play private app** | **Very likely skips**: Play-delivered apps are known to Play by definition | Different signing and update model (see below) | Inferred; no observational report found |
| Developer verification and package/key registration | **Does not skip**; affects only the identity gate | Free for Play Console accounts | Official (verification docs never mention Play Protect) + Anecdotal ([OpenAmpere #608](https://github.com/Gr33ndev/OpenAmpere/issues/608)) |
| Play Protect appeal | **Does not skip** the unknown-app prompt; can reverse a wrong PHA/MUwS verdict | Applies per verdict, apparently per hash | Official ([warning guidance](https://developers.google.com/android/play-protect/warning-dev-guidance)) + Anecdotal |
| Avoiding SMS, notification-listener and accessibility permissions; current targetSdk; valid APK/ZIP | **Avoids hard blocks**; the scan still happens | Free | Official |
| "Improve harmful app detection" | Automates uploads; **does not suppress** the prompt | n/a | Official + Anecdotal |
| `setRequireUserAction(USER_ACTION_NOT_REQUIRED)`, update ownership, `setInstallerPackageName` | **No effect** on verification | n/a | Official (AOSP) |

Two of the skipping routes need more detail. On managed devices, Android Management API policy offers only `VERIFY_APPS_ENFORCED` (the default) or `VERIFY_APPS_USER_CHOICE` for Play Protect; **no policy value disables it** ([AMAPI policies reference](https://developers.google.com/android/management/reference/rest/v1/enterprises.policies)). So the managed-device route works through Google's prompt suppression for work profiles and managed devices, not through an "off" setting. The same policy's `untrustedAppsPolicy` defaults to `DISALLOW_INSTALL`, so an installer running inside the work profile would need `ALLOW_INSTALL_DEVICE_WIDE` (inferred from the reference). The Play-installer routes rest on mechanism, not testimony. Google says the prompt targets apps it "hasn't seen", and a build delivered by the Play Store has been ingested by Play. But the notes found no Google sentence and no community test confirming it. One more framework fact matters here. The verification broadcast tells the verifier the *real* initiating package and UID, not the spoofable `setInstallerPackageName` value ([AOSP VerifyingSession](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/main/services/core/java/com/android/server/pm/VerifyingSession.java)). An installer therefore cannot pretend to be Play.

## Each new build restarts the scan, and the pass-the-scan levers are weak

The "make it pass" category is thinner than blog advice suggests. Google's only instruction for the unknown-app prompts is to "make sure Google Play Protect is aware of your app", and it never says how ([warning guidance](https://developers.google.com/android/play-protect/warning-dev-guidance)). Third-party guides claim that uploading the build to an internal test track, even as a draft, makes Play Protect recognise the key ([tutorialpedia](https://www.tutorialpedia.org/blog/installation-app-blocked-by-play-protect/), [ptkd.com](https://ptkd.com/journal/blocked-by-play-protect-warning-fix)). Google does not confirm this, and the Harbor maintainers listed it as an unconfirmed fix in November 2026 ([harbor #88](https://github.com/RDevNeo/harbor/issues/88)). Play App Signing also undermines the theory. Play re-signs uploads with Google's app signing key, so any "familiarity" Play builds up plausibly attaches to the Play-signed artifact. A directly distributed APK signed with the upload key differs in both certificate and hash ([appstestlab](https://appstestlab.com/guides/play-console/play-app-signing-and-key-management)). Whether one user's scan clears the prompt for other devices is also undocumented. Priming is therefore an experiment, not a plan: scan build N on device A, then install the identical file on device B with a different account.

Appeals are narrower still. The form at support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/contact/protectappeals can reverse a mistaken PHA or MUwS classification. Google says it is "not relevant" to the "Send app for security check" warning ([warning guidance](https://developers.google.com/android/play-protect/warning-dev-guidance)). One developer reported in November 2026 that the form asks for the APK's SHA-256 *as uploaded to VirusTotal*. The same report says it caps free text at 1,000 characters, warns "you will not receive a response", and that the next version was blocked again ([OpenAmpere #608](https://github.com/Gr33ndev/OpenAmpere/issues/608), anecdotal). Treat an appeal as a fix for one file, not a lasting allowlist. No general developer-facing allowlist for internal apps on unmanaged devices exists. The one allowlisting path Google staff have described is for blocked device-policy-controller (DPC) apps, and admins in that thread had not seen it work by February 2026 ([Android Enterprise Community](https://www.androidenterprise.community/android-enterprise-general-discussions-3/play-protect-is-blocking-our-dpc-app-appeal-already-submitted-looking-for-guidance-1569)).

The levers that do work target the policy gate. Google's guidance is to avoid SMS, notification-listener and accessibility permissions (SMS Retriever or User Consent APIs instead of `READ_SMS`), to keep targetSdk within two levels of the device's API level, and to ship well-formed APK/ZIP headers ([warning guidance](https://developers.google.com/android/play-protect/warning-dev-guidance)). Following them prevents the fraud block, the "compatibility too low" warning and some harmful-app verdicts. None of them stops the "unseen app" prompt.

## Developer verification is live in four countries but does not touch Play Protect

As of 15 November 2026, verification is enforced **only for installs from seven participating stores**: Google Play, Galaxy Store, and the HONOR, OPPO, vivo, Transsion and Xiaomi stores. Enforcement started on 30 September 2026, and only in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand ([Android Developers Blog, June 2026](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2026/06/android-developer-verification.html)). Google's FAQ is explicit that if users "sideload your app directly, these new verification requirements won't apply to your app yet." Global expansion covering all apps on certified Android 7+ devices is set for **2027**, with no exact date or country order announced ([verification FAQ](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification/guides/faq?hl=en), [landing page](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification)). The escape hatches that shipped are permanent ADB installs with no waiting period and, from August 2026, the "advanced flow". That flow takes a one-time 24-hour wait plus re-authentication and keeps showing an unverified-developer warning ([FAQ](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification/guides/faq?hl=en), [9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/08/18/google-gradually-rolling-out-androids-advanced-sideloading-ahead-of-developer-verification/)). Updates to unregistered apps fail once the advanced flow is off, except over ADB.

Verification matters to this developer for future-proofing, not for the scan. Play Console identities are verified automatically, and Play App Signing apps are registered automatically *with the app signing key* ([FAQ](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification/guides/faq?hl=en)). A direct-channel key, or a direct-only package, has to be added by hand. In Play Console, open Android developer verification, then the Package names tab, then Register package name, and give the SHA-256 certificate. Ownership of an existing package is proved by building a release APK containing `assets/adi-registration.properties` with Google's snippet, signed with that private key, and uploading it ([Play Console Help 16761053](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16761053?hl=en)). One developer confirmed that the challenge APK can be signed locally and never published ([OpenAmpere #608](https://github.com/Gr33ndev/OpenAmpere/issues/608)). Multiple keys per package are supported, and the Android Developer Console API allows registration from CI. The same report is the strongest evidence that verification does nothing for Play Protect. A fully verified, registered app passed the `VerificationCheck` and still drew "App scan recommended" and then "Harmful app blocked".

## A recommended setup for Release Hub on personal devices

The cleanest arrangement avoids running two channels for the same package on the same device. **Apps that also ship on Play should be installed on your phones from the internal testing track.** That track allows up to 100 testers and makes releases available "within minutes" ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9845334)). Those installs carry the Play app signing key, update from Play, and very likely never prompt. Release Hub can still *list* these apps, but it cannot update them. Their signature belongs to Google's key, so a direct-channel build signed with the upload key fails with a signature mismatch, and the Play Store holds installer status. For the same package on the direct channel, choose one of two options. You can give direct builds a distinct `applicationId` suffix such as `.direct` so the two channels coexist. Or you can self-host the Play-signed universal APK from App bundle explorer; that path is unverified, and it breaks if automatic protection is on, because the Play-protected build tells off-Play users to "get your app on Google Play" ([automatic protection](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10183279)). Internal app sharing is fine for one-off test links, but its per-app re-signing key isolates those installs from every other channel ([internal app sharing](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9844679)).

**For direct-only apps on a personal phone you want to keep protected, run Release Hub inside a work profile.** Create one through a free Android Management API enterprise or a minimal DPC, set `untrustedAppsPolicy` to `ALLOW_INSTALL_DEVICE_WIDE`, and install Release Hub in the profile. Every app it installs or updates there then falls under Google's "prompt will not appear in Work Profile" rule, while on-device malware detection keeps running ([Android Enterprise Help](https://support.google.com/work/android/answer/15162069)). This is the only documented route that removes the prompt without weakening the device. Whether a profile created by a hobbyist DPC counts is inferred, not confirmed. **On dedicated test phones, turning off "Scan apps with Play Protect" is the blunt alternative.** It is official and immediate, but it removes protection for everything installed on that phone, so keep it off daily drivers. **During development, install over ADB with `verifier_verify_adb_installs 0`**, which skips both Play Protect and, once enforced, the verification gate.

Finally, **register every package name and every signing key now**. That means the direct-channel key for each direct package, plus the upload key or local key for any package that Play has already auto-registered with the app signing key. Registration costs a few minutes per key, and without it direct updates break when verification goes global in 2027.

## Implications for the Release Hub installer app itself

Release Hub's installer, `mobile/android/app/src/main/kotlin/groot/mobile/install/SessionInstaller.kt`, sets `USER_ACTION_NOT_REQUIRED`, and `InstallController` runs background auto-updates that report through notifications. Neither setting reaches the verifier. **Expect background updates of direct-channel apps to hit "App scan recommended" whenever the build is new to Play Protect, which is potentially every release.** Harbor's maintainers responded by committing installs only while the app is in the foreground, so the user can tap "Scan app" ([harbor #88](https://github.com/RDevNeo/harbor/issues/88)). No source documents what the verifier does when the installing app is in the background. It might show its UI anyway, wait until `verifier_timeout`, or fail. Release Hub should therefore treat silent auto-update as best-effort. Concretely, it should defer background commits on devices where Play Protect is on and the work profile is not in use, or turn them into a "tap to install" notification that commits in the foreground. It should also log the status and message of every background session so the actual behaviour shows up on your own devices. The existing `waitMillis` timeout that maps to `WaitingForUser` is the right fallback for a session that stalls behind a verifier dialog.

The bigger risk is **Release Hub's own APK**. Its manifest declares `REQUEST_INSTALL_PACKAGES`, `UPDATE_PACKAGES_WITHOUT_USER_ACTION`, `REQUEST_DELETE_PACKAGES` and `QUERY_ALL_PACKAGES`. That is a superset of the pair OpenAmpere's maintainer pinned down as the trigger for "Harmful app blocked" on a verified, registered app. Removing the self-updater made the next version install cleanly ([OpenAmpere #608](https://github.com/Gr33ndev/OpenAmpere/issues/608)). This is one anecdote, but the mechanism is plausible: an unknown, sideloaded app that silently installs other apps is exactly what malware droppers look like. Release Hub cannot drop these permissions without ceasing to be an installer. The mitigations are therefore about where the app lives. Install it into the managed work profile; install it over ADB on development devices; and be cautious about having it update *itself* through a session, since each self-update is a fresh, unseen hash with the riskiest permission profile. If it is flagged, the appeal path is per-hash and gives no feedback. Two smaller points round this out. Release Hub declares none of the SMS, notification-listener or accessibility permissions that trigger the fraud block, so it should stay that way. And the `setPackageSource(PACKAGE_SOURCE_STORE)` call identifies the session as coming from a store, but the verifier sees Release Hub's real package and UID, and nothing documents that this changes Play Protect's view.

## Conclusion

The real decision is not how to convince Play Protect to trust your APKs. It is which installer and which profile each app arrives through. Google has deliberately put every skip lever on the *device* side: the user toggle, ADB, enterprise management and Play as installer. Every *developer*-side lever, including verification, registration, appeals and permission hygiene, only shapes the verdict. For a single developer the cheapest durable answer is structural. Play-tracked apps update from Play, direct apps update from Release Hub inside a work profile, and the direct key is registered before 2027.

The open questions are empirical and cheap to settle on your own hardware. Does a Play-internal-track install ever prompt? Does a scan on one device clear the next device? Does a background session hang or fail behind the verifier? Does Release Hub's own APK draw a harmful-app verdict when sideloaded onto a clean Android 15 device? Answers from a few test devices would be firmer than anything currently published.
